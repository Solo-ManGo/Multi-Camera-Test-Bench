Welcome everyone. I'm Slava Mazgo. Today we are reviewing the architecture and validation strategy for our custom ADAS Central ECU built around the Mobileye EyeQ6H platform. This review covers hardware integration, the GMSL3 data pipeline, and our Hardware-in-the-Loop approach.

Executive Summary & Project Goals

Primary Objective

Validate a custom ADAS Central ECU built around the Mobileye EyeQ6H SoC driving a comprehensive 11-camera surround vision suite.

The Technical Challenge

Ingesting multiple high-resolution uncompressed camera streams concurrently without packet loss, latency spikes, or signal degradation over the vehicle harness.

The Chosen Backbone

Standardizing completely on Analog Devices GMSL3 to deliver the mandatory bandwidth, virtual channel handling (up to 16 VCs), and low latency demanded by Mobileye vision accelerators.

Test Bench Purpose

  • Functional verification of the physical PCB.
  • Power-over-Coax (PoC) robustness under load.
  • Signal Integrity (SI) characterization.
  • EyeQ Kit SDK software stack testing under realistic thermal/electrical scenarios.
Our technical challenge is immense: moving 11 raw camera feeds simultaneously into a single ECU. We have standardized on GMSL3 over FPD-Link due to its seamless integration with the EyeQ platform and its PAM4 12Gbps throughput. This bench will validate everything from board traces to the Linux Device Tree.

System Topology & Chipset Selection

Multi-Deserializer Matrix: Deploying three Analog Devices MAX96792A ICs in parallel.
Link Optimization: GMSL3 dual-input tunneling yields 6 high-bandwidth coaxial ports for 11 zones + expansion.
Host Pipeline: Outputting raw video to EyeQ6H NVCSI via dual 4-lane MIPI CSI-2 links.
Notice how we handle the bandwidth: We use three MAX96792A deserializers. Because GMSL3 uses PAM4 tunneling, each IC can ingest multiple camera streams on a single coax. This perfectly maps our Front, Side, and Rear arrays, feeding directly into the EyeQ6H's MIPI receivers.

Direct Video Injection Data Flow

Bypassing the camera’s image sensor and lens completely. Generating bit-accurate RAW video data and injecting it directly into the ADAS ECU's SerDes interface. The ECU cannot distinguish between the injection rig and a physical camera.

1. The Data Pipeline

  • Simulation Host: Outputs uncompressed frames via PCIe.
  • FPGA Board: Converts frames to MIPI CSI-2, matching sensor pixel formats exactly.
  • Serializer: Automotive PHY chip converting parallel MIPI into a high-speed RF stream.
  • Interconnect: Heavy-shielded cables straight into ECU deserializers.

2. Critical Integration Blockers

  • I2C Back-Channel Emulation: FPGA must run a Slave Emulator to spoof camera registers and prevent "Camera Lost" drops.
  • PoC Management: The board must safely sink 9V-12V DC power from the ECU (dummy load) to avoid frying hardware.
  • Frame Synchronization: Must intercept ECU hardware FSYNC pulses to lock simulated frame release.
Pointing cameras at screens is flawed due to Moire and dynamic range limits. Instead, we use Direct Video Injection. The FPGA takes simulated data from PCIe, formats it as MIPI, and pushes it through a real GMSL serializer. The hardest parts are not the video—it's spoofing the I2C control channel, managing PoC voltages, and syncing to the ECU's hardware clock.

Physical Test Bench Hardware Architecture

The Device Under Test (DUT): Custom EyeQ6H carrier hub board mounted to a thermal management plate.

Video Feed Injection (Two Operational Configurations)

Option A: Real-World Sensor Arrays

A physical rig mounting the actual 11 automotive camera modules (e.g., Onsemi/Sony) connected via 10-meter coaxial cables.

Option B: HIL Video Simulator (Validation Mode)

Digital PCIe frame grabber/simulator running an ADAS environment, feeding virtual streams into 11 discrete GMSL3 hardware serializers.

Interconnect Standards:
Enforcing the use of Rosenberger HMTD® / Amphenol Mini-FAKRA multiport connectors across the bench to guarantee signal matching.
Power Subsystem:
Dedicated programmable laboratory power supplies mimicking automotive battery fluctuations (9V to 16V transient blocks).
Our bench supports two modes: connecting the physical automotive cameras on a rig, or swapping those out for the HIL video injection system we just discussed. In both cases, we mandate Mini-FAKRA connectors and use programmable power supplies to simulate alternator noise and voltage drops.

Addressing GMSL3 Physical Layer Challenges

PAM4 Signaling Constraints

GMSL3 transitions from binary NRZ to 4-level Pulse Amplitude Modulation (PAM4) to reach 12 Gbps per link. The test bench must actively validate the highly compressed noise margins associated with PAM4.

Power-Over-Coax (PoC) Isolation
  • Integrates precise inline current-monitoring shunts on each coaxial link.
  • Validates that the multi-stage PoC inductor/ferrite filtering array successfully isolates the 12 Gbps video line from high-frequency DC/DC regulator switching noise.
Signal Integrity (SI) Verification
  • Integration of high-bandwidth oscilloscope probing points directly on the differential pairs.
  • Target metric: Complete eye-diagram clearance and zero link-drops over extended 48-hour continuous testing loops.
Because GMSL3 pushes 12 Gigabits per second, it uses PAM4 modulation. The voltage steps are tiny. If our Power-over-Coax filter isn't perfect, regulator switching noise will destroy the data eye. We have designed probing points directly on the differential pairs to validate the eye diagrams mechanically.

Software Stack Verification & EyeQ Kit Integration

The Control Loop (I2C Address Translation)

All 11 cameras arrive with identical factory base I2C addresses (e.g., 0x30). Bench validates the MAX96792A's hardware translation registers mapping unique, runtime aliases cleanly across the EyeQ6H master bus.

Linux Device Tree (DTS) Mapping

Verification of virtual channel parameters written via EyeQ Kit SDK:

&nvcsi_port0 { status = "okay"; vc_id = <0 1 2 3>; /* VC routing */ remote-endpoint = <&max96792a_out>; };

Ensures DTS properly instantiates four virtual channels mapped to each physical NVCSI port.

Hardware is only half the battle. On the software side, we use the EyeQ Kit SDK. The test bench validates that our I2C address translation works so the bus doesn't collide. We also verify the Linux Device Tree bindings to ensure the Video4Linux2 pipeline passes the data properly to the NVCSI host.

Test Bench Test Cases & Validation Matrix
